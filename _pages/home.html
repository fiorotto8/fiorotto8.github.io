Making particles visible

A particle crossing a gas can leave a trail of electrons. We guide those electrons with an electric field, amplify their signal and photograph the light produced. This is the idea behind an optical time projection chamber, or TPC: an image from which we can reconstruct a particle’s track.

CYGNO04: looking for the direction of dark matter

CYGNO04 is a 400 L demonstrator at the Gran Sasso National Laboratory (LNGS). We image tiny recoil tracks in gas to look for the directional pattern expected from dark matter as the Solar System moves through the Galactic halo.

If a dark matter particle scatters from an atomic nucleus in the gas, the recoiling nucleus could leave a tiny track. Across many events, those directions could help distinguish a dark matter signal from ordinary radiation. The animation shows why our motion through the halo gives an apparent incoming “wind”.

The demonstrator brings optical tracking into a larger detector for rare-event searches underground. Alongside reconstructing tracks, we need to understand the detector’s response and the backgrounds that can imitate the events we are looking for.

Building and sharing the experiment

As Technical Coordinator, I coordinate detector assembly, gas and high-voltage systems, readout and laboratory services, working with the team from procurement through commissioning.

I also designed and manage the CYGNO website and manage @cygno.exp on Instagram, where we share the experiment and the work behind it.

What the job looks like day to day

Some of my work is very hands-on: assembling a detector, checking a gas system or getting a prototype ready to take data. Some of it is coordinating the people and services needed for the next stage of the experiment.

Once there are data, the questions change. How much light did the detector produce? How stable is its response? Does the image agree with the simulation? I work on calibration and response models so that we can relate what the camera records to what happened in the gas.

X-rays have more to tell us

An X-ray carries information in its polarisation: the orientation of its electric-field oscillation. Measuring that property can help us study the physical processes that produced the radiation.

When an X-ray is absorbed in the gas, it can release an electron. The initial directions of many of these electrons carry a signature of the X-rays’ polarisation. My work is to reconstruct those small, often curved tracks from camera images.

One track is not enough. In the animation, many simulated tracks gradually build up a preferred orientation. That statistical pattern is what lets us estimate polarisation; the detector’s own response must also be understood.

Within the HypeX project, I develop and test optical TPCs for hard X-ray polarimetry, with the aim of extending sensitivity across 10–60 keV. This connects detector technology developed for dark matter searches with questions in high-energy astrophysics.

From a prototype to a measurement

A useful detector needs a response we understand. I test prototypes, calibrate them with X-rays and compare reconstructed tracks with simulations. That comparison helps us separate the physics we want to measure from the effects of the gas, amplification and camera.

Further questions: neutrinos and space

Neutrino interactions

I also study detector concepts for directional solar neutrinos and coherent elastic neutrino–nucleus scattering (CEνNS), in which a neutrino transfers momentum to a nucleus. These are developing research directions.

EXPO: background in orbit

For the EXPO mission proposal, I coordinate simulations of the instrument’s background in orbit: the unwanted signals produced by the radiation surrounding a spacecraft. Understanding those signals is part of designing an instrument that can pick out its astrophysical targets.

Earlier work: PICOSEC & CMS GEM

PICOSEC: measuring arrival time

Knowing precisely when a particle arrives helps separate useful signals from background. I coordinated PICOSEC Micromegas research and development for muon-collider applications, working on prototypes, photocathodes, radiators and test beams, alongside simulations of beam-induced backgrounds.

CMS GEM: making detectors reliable

A detector also has to keep working in a demanding environment. I worked on the CMS muon-system upgrade using gas electron multipliers (GEMs), from quality control to commissioning and Run 3 operations. My responsibilities included gas systems and studies of ageing, electrical discharges and operation in magnetic fields.

Teaching & outreach

I teach detector laboratories, supervise student projects and talk about our work with people outside the field.

Learning with a real detector

I lecture on low-energy radiation measurement at GSSI and tutor at the DRD1 and RD51 detector schools at CERN, and the Gran Sasso Hands-on PhD Summer School.

In a detector school, students can change a voltage and watch what happens to a signal. I help them connect what they measure at the bench to the underlying physics.

With students

I supervise or co-supervise projects at bachelor's, master's and PhD level, as well as CERN Summer Student and internship projects.

Scientific service

I help organise detector schools and collaboration meetings, and referee for JINST and EPJ Techniques and Instrumentation.

Outside the research group

I guide visitors at Gran Sasso, give public seminars, take part in European Researchers' Night and coordinate CYGNO outreach and social-media activities. I previously guided visitors at CMS.

About me

I studied physics at the University of Pavia, where I completed my PhD in 2022. My work on the CMS GEM detectors took me to CERN; since 2023, I've been based at GSSI in L'Aquila.

I’m now a postdoctoral researcher at GSSI, where I divide my research between CYGNO and X-ray polarimetry.
